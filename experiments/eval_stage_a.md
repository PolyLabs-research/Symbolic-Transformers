# Formula evaluation, Stage A: results

**Status: complete. Both formats fail every pre-registered threshold.**

The task, thresholds and predictions are in `plan_formula_evaluation.md`. They were committed in `eed18e0`
before any model was trained. Numbers come from `python scripts/assess_eval.py`, with per-split detail in
`eval_stage_a.json`. The data is regenerated by `python data/eval_generator.py` (seed 0; `metadata.json`
holds each split's sha256).

## What was run

- **Pre-registered.** `tiny` (569K parameters) in each format: batch 64, learning rate 3e-4, 500
  warm-up steps, 30 epochs, seed 1, on CPU.
- **Interruption.** The container was shut down for inactivity at epoch 15 (direct) and 13 (scratchpad).
  Both runs resumed from those checkpoints with optimizer and schedule state intact.
- **Validation loss.**
  - Direct: never left chance (ln 2 = 0.693). Its best was 0.692 at epoch 8, and it finished at 0.711
    while training loss crept to 0.672.
  - Scratchpad: per-target-token loss fell from 1.05 to 0.113, a new best at every epoch.
- **Control added after the fact** (not pre-registered). The direct format trained for 10 epochs,
  starting from the scratchpad model's weights. Validation loss went from 0.695 to 0.673.

## Why: the model composes but cannot look facts up

Teacher-forced accuracy of the scratchpad model by kind of target token (first 400 items of each split):

| split | structure (copying) | lookups (step 0) | lookup: atom present | lookup: atom absent | reductions (later steps) | answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| test_iid | 100.0 | 59.5 | 73.3 | 46.3 | 96.7 | 100.0 |
| test_shift | 99.0 | 67.4 | 69.4 | 65.1 | 97.1 | 100.0 |
| test_negation | 100.0 | 57.1 | 66.1 | 47.1 | 97.7 | 100.0 |
| test_large_world | 75.7 | 50.2 | 83.2 | 15.9 | 77.3 | 96.2 |
| test_deep | 70.2 | 45.9 | 45.6 | 46.3 | 71.4 | 90.8 |

1. **Composition is learned.**
   - Given a correct previous step, the model evaluates the innermost operators correctly 97% of the
     time.
   - It copies the formula's structure perfectly, and the answer from the last step.
   - Negation of compound formulas is no harder than anything else (97.7%).
2. **Lookups are not learned.** Deciding whether `PRED p ( CONST a )` is among the listed true atoms is
   close to chance everywhere. The model leans towards TRUE more the longer the world's list is: absent
   atoms are right 46% of the time in 2-4-object worlds and 16% in 5-6-object worlds.
   - The shift split's 67% comes from its extreme densities (0.2 and 0.8), where a world's length
     predicts its atoms' values.
   - Very short worlds may be an exception: the 17 test_iid items of at most 50 tokens score 83%. That
     is too few items to rely on.
3. **One wrong lookup is usually fatal.**
   - Step 0 is exactly right on 33% of test_iid items, and accuracy there is 52.9%.
   - Every test item is half of a minimal pair, so its value hinges on an atom, and a lookup error
     usually flips the answer.
   - Pair accuracy (10.5%) is far below the surface MLP's (79.5%), and the MLP is handed the true count
     of each formula's atoms as a feature.
4. **The direct format has nothing to compose without lookups,** and the warm start had no lookups to
   inherit. So it stays at chance with or without the scratchpad's weights.
5. **Past the training lengths everything breaks.**
   - Training sequences have a median of 130 tokens and a 95th percentile of 212; the longest is 315.
   - On test_deep and test_large_world, teacher-forced structure copying drops to 70-76%.
   - Greedy accuracy on test_deep falls to 8% for sequences of 401-450 tokens and 0% beyond, and 3% of
     its items never reach an answer.
   - These are absolute sinusoidal positions that training rarely or never reached. The depth and
     world-size splits therefore cannot separate depth from length until the length failure is fixed.

## What Stage A tells us

The pre-registered tests asked whether a tiny transformer evaluates propositional formulas against a
world. In this setup it does not, but the failure is specific.

- **The composition half works.** Level-by-level reduction is learned almost perfectly in distribution.
- **What breaks is looking a fact up** in an unordered list of true atoms, and anything past the trained
  sequence lengths.
- **So Stage A has not yet tested formula evaluation.** The bottleneck sits upstream of it, in reading
  the world.

Two changes target those failures, and either could be tested cheaply before rerunning Stage A:

- **Make lookups a copy, not a membership test.** List every possible atom with its value
  (`PRED p ( CONST a ) TRUE ...`), instead of only the true ones. Looking up an atom then means finding
  the matching atom and copying the token after it, the induction-head pattern that two-layer
  transformers learn readily. The current encoding instead asks the model to detect whether something is
  absent from a list. A quick diagnostic on lookup-only items (height 0) would compare the two encodings
  in tiny and small models.
- **Train on the lengths you test on.** Either cover the test lengths in training, or randomise positions
  during training: random offsets for the absolute positions, as in Ruoss et al. 2023, "Randomized
  Positional Encodings Boost Length Generalization of Transformers". Otherwise the deep and large-world
  splits measure position extrapolation, not reasoning.

The world encoding was agreed for this plan, so changing it is a decision for the project. These results
are the case for doing so.
